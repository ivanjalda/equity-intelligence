Learn to read the analysis

Last updated: November 14, 2026

This guide explains, step by step, what you are looking at on each screen and what each term means. You do not need to know anything about the stock market. It is educational content and not investment advice.

The numbers in the examples on this page are made up and only serve to explain. The real data is in the market analysis.
1. Look at the verdictIt is the summary: BUY, REVIEW, WAIT or EXPENSIVE.
2. Look at whyValue, margin of safety and agreement between methods.
3. Look at health and reliabilityA cheap but fragile company is not an opportunity.
4. You decideIt is information. The decision and the risk are yours.

Start here

What you see on a stock's screen and what each verdict means.

How to read a stock's screen

When you choose a company in the Market analysis you see a row of boxes. Made-up example:

BoxExampleWhat it means
Price$100What a share costs at the last close.
Intrinsic value$160What we estimate it is worth, averaging our methods.
Margin of safety37.5%How far below the value the price is: (160 − 100) ÷ 160.
Agreement between methodsHighWhether the methods give similar results. “High” gives more confidence; “Low” calls for caution.
P/E14xHow many years of current earnings you pay for the stock.

Below it appears a box with the conclusion in one sentence and, if it is not BUY, the recommended entry price: the price at which the margin of safety would reach 25%.

The four verdicts

BUYIt trades at least 25% below its value and has no warning signs. This is the zone of interest.
REVIEWIt looks cheap, but the data has something odd. Look at the reliability seal to see what.
WAITIt is not cheap yet. You get the entry price and a date to look again.
EXPENSIVEIt trades above its calculated value. It does not mean it will fall, only that there is no margin of safety.

The chart

It shows the evolution of the price. On valued stocks you will see two dashed lines: the intrinsic value and the entry price (25% below). If the price is below the entry line, it is in the buy zone. You can change the period (1 month, 6 months, year to date, 1 year, 5 years or the full history).

If the price has fallen more than 35% from its high of the last year, a warning appears: sometimes the market sees something the accounts do not yet reflect, and it is wise to understand the reason.

How we work out what it is worth

The valuation methods, the agreement between them and the margin of safety.

The three methods, one by one

1. Discounted cash flow

It starts from a simple idea: a company is worth the cash it will have left over in the future, brought to today's price. We estimate that cash over 10 years using the growth the company has had (limited between 0% and 12%), and discount it with each company's own rate: the most volatile demand more return and the most stable less (10% if there is no data to measure it). It is the most “textbook” method, but also the most sensitive: a small change in growth or in the discount moves the result a lot.

2. Graham formula

Benjamin Graham, Warren Buffett's teacher, devised a quick formula: earnings per share × (8.5 + 2 × growth) adjusted by the yield on quality bonds. It is conservative and serves as a contrast.

3. Historical multiples

It looks at how many times earnings the market has paid for this company over the years (median P/E, limited between 8 and 30) and applies it to current earnings. It answers: “how much would it be worth if the market valued it again as it usually does?”.

Agreement between methods

If the three methods give similar numbers, there is high agreement and more confidence. If one gives $100 and another $300, there is low agreement: one of the assumptions fails and it is wise to distrust the average value.

Margin of safety and entry price

A large margin is a cushion, but it can also be a warning sign. If the price has dropped sharply and the margin shoots up, sometimes the market knows something the past accounts do not yet reflect (a business problem, a new competitor, a technological change). An intrinsic value is calculated with yesterday's data; the price incorporates tomorrow's expectations. That is why a very high margin carries a warning on the company page: before acting, understand why it trades so far below.

P/E and other ratios on the company page

  • P/E: price ÷ earnings per share. A P/E of 14 means you pay 14 years of current earnings. Low is not always good (earnings may be about to fall); high is not always bad (it may be growing fast).
  • Earnings growth (5 years): the average pace at which earnings per share have grown. It is the basis of the growth we use in the methods.
  • Free cash flow yield: free cash per share ÷ price. It is like the “interest” the company would give you in real money if it paid out all its cash.

Company quality and risk

Financial health, data reliability, time horizons and companies that rise and fall with the economy.

Company health

It summarizes 5 years of official accounts with small bars. Five things are looked at:

  • Revenue: what it sells. Rising is a good sign.
  • Earnings: what it makes after all expenses.
  • Free cash: the money left after paying expenses and investments. It is hard to disguise.
  • Debt: what it owes. If it grows much faster than the business, it is an alarm.
  • Equity: what it has after subtracting debts.

Red bars are years with a negative value. The final label says Solid, Acceptable or Fragile. A fragile company cannot come out as BUY, even if it looks cheap.

Reliability seal

It answers “can I trust these numbers?”. We check that the accounts are recent, that there is enough history, that earnings per share match total earnings, that the P/E is reasonable and that the methods agree. The more checks passed, the higher the seal (High, Medium or Low). With low reliability, the verdict drops to REVIEW.

Short, medium and long term

It is a guideline label that says how much patience may be needed:

  • Short: cheap and also below its average of the last 200 sessions. It usually indicates it has fallen recently.
  • Long: stable (non-cyclical) business with earnings in each of the last 5 years and sustained growth.
  • Medium: the rest.

It is not a prediction of when the calculated value will be reached. The market can take years to recognize it, or never do so.

Cyclical companies

They are those that depend heavily on the economy: oil, mining, construction, cars, airlines… In a good year they earn a lot and in a bad one they can lose. That is why their value is calculated with the average of several years and the “cyclical” label appears. You can filter them with the Cyclical only button.

Beyond stocks

Bonds, commodities, indices, large funds and the signal history.

Bonds

A bond is a loan: you lend money to a government or a company and it pays you interest. What matters:

  • Yield (or return): what they pay per year relative to their price.
  • When the yield rises, the bond price falls, and vice versa. The longer the term, the more it moves.
  • 10-year percentile: compares today's yield with that of the last 10 years. “90th percentile” means today it is higher than 90% of the days of that decade. When it is in the highest 25%, our analysis says BUY.

Corporate bond funds are also measured by the credit spread: what they pay over Treasuries. If it is high, the market sees more risk.

Commodities

Gold, silver, oil, copper, grains… They do not generate earnings, so they cannot be valued like a company. We compare their inflation-adjusted price with their entire history: in the lowest 25% it comes out BUY and in the highest 25%, EXPENSIVE. Without adjusting for inflation, almost everything would look expensive today. It is an indicator of whether the price is high or low in perspective, not a prediction.

Indexes and ETFs

An index measures a set of companies (for example, the S&P 500). An ETF is an exchange-traded fund that replicates that index, so you can buy it like a stock. To value them we add up the value of the companies we have valued: if the set trades 15% or more below, it comes out BUY. It tells you whether the whole market is expensive or cheap.

Large funds (13F reports)

Large US managers file with the SEC every quarter what they hold in their portfolio. In the Large funds section you see what they hold, what they have bought and what they have sold.

  • They arrive up to 45 days late: by the time you read them, they may have already changed.
  • They include only US stocks and do not reflect short sales.
  • A large investor holding a stock does not make it good: they have different goals, a different time horizon and a different size than you.

The track record: how to read it

Each signal is saved on the day it is issued, with its price, and is not edited. It is then compared with the S&P 500, the Nasdaq 100 and the total US market over the same dates. To read it well:

  • Look at how many signals have been measured. With few, the percentages prove nothing.
  • An average can mislead: also look at how many signals we beat the benchmark in.
  • Time matters: a signal from a week ago says almost nothing about a strategy designed for years.

Basic concepts

What each type of asset is, and a checklist to review before investing.

Stocks, bonds, commodities, ETFs: what each thing is

StocksA piece of a company. You gain if the company grows or pays dividends. Risk: the company may do badly.
BondsA loan. You collect interest and get your money back at maturity. Risk: rates rise or the issuer does not pay.
CommoditiesGold, oil… They give no earnings. Their price depends on supply, demand and inflation.
ETFs and indexesA basket of assets bought as if it were a single stock. It reduces the risk of picking the wrong company, but it follows the market.

Before investing: a checklist

  1. Invest only money you will not need in the short term.
  2. Do not put everything in one company: diversification reduces risk.
  3. Understand what the company lives on. If you do not understand it, do not buy it.
  4. Check the costs and taxes in your country and with your broker.
  5. A verdict is a starting point. Cross-check it with other sources.
  6. You can lose part or all of what you invested. Read the risk warning.

A company's accounts, line by line

On each company's page you see its official accounts for the last few years (from the SEC, the US regulator). Here we explain what each line is, with its accounting definition and, below it, in plain words. There are three reports: the income statement (what happened in the year), the balance sheet (the snapshot of what it owns and owes) and the cash flow statement (the real money that moved). Figures are in millions of dollars, except earnings per share, which are in dollars.

Income statement

It records a period's revenue and expenses (normally one year) and ends in profit or loss. It is read from top to bottom: each line subtracts expenses until reaching net income.

TermWhat it is
RevenueAmount of sales of goods and services provided by the company during the year, before subtracting any expense. Also called sales or turnover.
In plain words: Everything it bills in the year.
Cost of salesDirect cost of the goods sold or services provided: raw materials, manufacturing and direct labor. It does not include marketing or office expenses.
In plain words: What it costs to produce or buy what it sells.
Gross profitDifference between revenue and cost of sales. It measures how much of sales is left before paying overhead expenses.
In plain words: What is left of each sale before paying the other expenses.
Research and developmentExpense for the year devoted to researching and developing new or improved products, processes or services. Not all companies report it separately.
In plain words: What it invests in creating new products. Important in technology and pharma.
Selling, general and administrativeSelling, marketing, administration and overhead expenses not directly tied to production: advertising, stores, administrative salaries, office rent.
In plain words: Advertising, offices and day-to-day salaries.
Operating incomeProfit from ordinary activity before interest and taxes. Also called operating profit or EBIT.
In plain words: What the business itself earns, unaffected by how it is financed or by taxes.
Interest expenseCost of borrowed financing for the year: interest on loans, bonds and other interest-bearing debt.
In plain words: What it pays each year for the money it owes.
Pre-tax incomeResult for the year after deducting all expenses and interest, before subtracting income tax.
In plain words: What is left right before paying taxes.
TaxesIncome tax accrued during the year: what the company owes the government on its earnings.
In plain words: What it pays the government on its profits.
Net incomeFinal result for the year, after deducting all expenses, interest and taxes. It is the profit that belongs to shareholders.
In plain words: The bottom line: what the company truly earns in the year.
Earnings per shareNet income divided by the average number of shares for the year. “Diluted” also includes shares that could be created through options or convertible bonds.
In plain words: The part of the profit that belongs to each share. It is the basis of the P/E.
Shares (millions)Weighted average number of diluted shares outstanding during the year, in millions.
In plain words: If it falls over the years, the company is buying back its shares and each one is worth a larger part of the business. If it rises, new shares are being issued and your share is diluted.

Balance sheet

It is a photograph of the company's situation on a date (the year-end). It shows what it owns (assets), what it owes (liabilities) and what is left for shareholders (equity). It always holds: assets = liabilities + equity.

TermWhat it is
CashCash and cash equivalents: money in bank accounts and very short-term, highly liquid investments (normally maturing within three months).
In plain words: The money it has available right now.
Short-term investmentsFinancial investments (securities, deposits) maturing in under a year that the company can easily convert into cash.
In plain words: Money placed for the short term. Together with cash it is its cushion.
Accounts receivableAmounts owed by customers for credit sales (trade receivables), net of the provision for possible non-payment.
In plain words: What customers still owe it. If it grows much faster than sales, it may be struggling to collect.
InventoriesStock: raw materials, work in progress and finished goods intended for sale.
In plain words: The stored merchandise waiting to be sold.
Current assetsAssets the company expects to convert into cash, sell or consume within a year (or within its normal operating cycle): cash, accounts receivable, inventories.
In plain words: What it can turn into money in under a year.
Property, plant and equipmentTangible long-lived assets (land, buildings, machinery, equipment) valued at cost less accumulated depreciation.
In plain words: Factories, stores and machines it works with for years.
GoodwillDifference between the price paid in an acquisition and the fair value of the identifiable net assets of the acquired company. It is not amortized, but it is reviewed every year in case it has lost value (impairment).
In plain words: What it overpaid when buying other companies for their brand, customers and team. If the purchase goes badly, it is reduced and subtracts from profit.
Total assetsSum of all the company's assets and rights: current and non-current assets.
In plain words: Everything it owns.
Accounts payableDebts to suppliers for purchases of goods and services pending payment (trade payables).
In plain words: What it owes its suppliers for purchases already received.
Short-term debtCurrent financial liabilities: loans, bonds and other interest-bearing debt maturing in under a year, including the portion of long-term debt due within that period.
In plain words: The debt that has to be repaid soon.
Current liabilitiesObligations payable within less than a year: suppliers, debt coming due, taxes and accrued expenses.
In plain words: Everything it must pay in under a year.
Long-term debtNon-current financial liabilities: loans, bonds and other interest-bearing debt maturing or payable in more than a year.
In plain words: Loans due in more than a year. They give time, but the interest has to be paid every year.
Total liabilitiesSum of all the company's obligations to third parties: current and non-current liabilities.
In plain words: Everything it owes.
Shareholders' equityThe part of the assets that belongs to shareholders once all liabilities are deducted. It includes contributed capital, reserves and retained earnings. It is calculated as assets minus liabilities.
In plain words: What would be left for shareholders if the company sold everything and paid everything it owes. Also called net worth or book value.

Cash flow

It shows the real money coming in and going out of the company during the year, split into operating, investing and financing activities. A profit can be “on paper” (sales not yet collected); cash flow shows what truly moved.

TermWhat it is
Operating cash flowCash generated by the company's ordinary activity during the year, after collections from customers and payments to suppliers, employees and taxes.
In plain words: The money the business generates from its normal activity.
Capital expenditure (capex)Payments for the purchase of fixed assets (factories, stores, equipment) to maintain or expand the company's capacity.
In plain words: What it spends on facilities and equipment. It appears as a cash outflow.
Investing cash flowNet cash coming in or going out from buying and selling assets, financial investments and businesses. A negative number indicates the company invested more than it divested.
In plain words: Negative is normal for companies that invest.
Dividends paidCash distributed to shareholders during the year as a return on their investment. In the table it appears as the amount paid.
In plain words: The money it pays out to its shareholders.
Share buybacksPayments for purchasing the company's own shares on the market (treasury stock). It reduces the number of shares outstanding.
In plain words: It buys its own shares: each one that remains is worth a larger part of the business.
Financing cash flowNet cash coming in or going out from obtaining or repaying loans, issuing or repurchasing shares and paying dividends.
In plain words: Negative usually means it is returning money to lenders and shareholders.
Free cash flowOperating cash flow minus capital expenditure (capex). It is the cash left available to pay dividends, repurchase shares, reduce debt or save.
In plain words: The money that truly is left over. It is the figure our discounted cash flow method relies on.

Ratios and scores on the company page

These are calculations we make by dividing some accounts by others to quickly see how the company is doing. They appear in italics on the company page.

TermWhat it is
Gross marginGross profit divided by revenue, as a percentage.
In plain words: How many cents are left of each dollar sold after paying what it costs to produce it.
Operating marginOperating income divided by revenue, as a percentage.
In plain words: How many cents of each dollar sold the business earns before interest and taxes.
Net marginNet income divided by revenue, as a percentage.
In plain words: How many cents of each dollar sold end up as final profit.
ROE (return on equity)Net income divided by shareholders' equity.
In plain words: How much the shareholders' money earns. A high, sustained ROE usually indicates a good business, but with a lot of debt it can be artificially inflated.
Current ratioCurrent assets divided by current liabilities. It measures the ability to pay short-term debts.
In plain words: If it is 2, it has $2 available in the short term for every $1 it must pay in under a year. Below 1 there is a risk of difficulty.
Debt to equityTotal financial debt (short and long term) divided by shareholders' equity.
In plain words: The higher it is, the more the company is financed by debt and the more it suffers if the business slows.
Interest coverageOperating income divided by interest expense.
In plain words: How many times it could pay its interest with what it earns. Above 5 is comfortable; below 2, tight.
Profitability and solvency scoresOur own indicators, from 0 to 100, calculated with the official accounts and fixed rules that are the same for all companies. The profitability one measures how efficiently it makes money; the solvency one, its ability to meet its debts.
In plain words: A quick grade. The rules can be seen one by one on the company page. They are not a recommendation.
Growth over 3, 5 and 10 yearsCompound annual growth rate between the latest year and the one 3, 5 or 10 years ago. It is only calculated when both values are positive.
In plain words: How much it has grown on average each year.
Bearish, base and bullish scenariosThree versions of intrinsic value. The base uses the normal assumptions. The bearish assumes 2 points less growth and 1 point more discount rate (and the company's own low P/E); the bullish, the opposite; also, if the company's earnings and cash are today clearly below their average of the last 5 years, the bullish case assumes they return to that average (capped at double the current figure).
In plain words: They show how much the value changes if things go worse or better than expected. The verdict is based on the base.
Company-specific discount rateThe minimum return we require from each company to compensate for its risk: the yield on the 10-year US Treasury bond plus beta × 5.5 points of risk premium. Beta measures how much the stock price moves relative to the market over the last 5 years.
In plain words: The most volatile demand more and the most stable less. If there are not enough prices to measure beta, we use a standard 10%.

Other accounting terms you will see on other websites

Not all of them appear on our company page, but you will find them in annual reports and on other financial sites.

TermWhat it is
Non-current assetsAssets and rights the company holds for more than a year: fixed assets, long-term investments, intangible assets, goodwill.
In plain words: What it owns and does not plan to turn into cash in the next year.
Non-current liabilitiesDebts and obligations of the company that mature or are payable in more than a year: long-term debt, long-term provisions, deferred taxes.
In plain words: What it must pay in more than a year.
Current and non-current financial liabilitiesInterest-bearing debts (loans, bonds, leases). They are current if they mature in under a year and non-current if they mature later.
In plain words: The debts “with interest”, separate from those owed to suppliers.
ProvisionsLiabilities of uncertain amount or date that the company estimates it will have to pay: lawsuits, warranties, restructurings, pensions.
In plain words: Money it sets aside in case it has to pay something that is not yet certain.
Working capitalCurrent assets minus current liabilities. It measures the short-term financial cushion.
In plain words: If it is positive, what it has in the short term exceeds what it owes in the short term.
Depreciation and amortizationSpreading the cost of a long-lived asset over the years of its useful life. It is an accounting expense that does not involve cash going out that year.
In plain words: The wear of machines and buildings, spread over years.
EBITDAEarnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization. It is used as an approximation of the cash the activity generates.
In plain words: What the business earns without counting the wear of its equipment. It is not real money: it ignores investments.
Net financial resultDifference between financial income (interest received, dividends from investments) and financial expenses (interest paid).
In plain words: What it gains or loses on its money and its debts, apart from the business.
Share capitalAmount contributed by shareholders when the company was formed and in capital increases, which is part of shareholders' equity.
In plain words: What the shareholders put in from the start.
Reserves and retained earningsProfits from previous years that the company has not distributed and keeps within shareholders' equity.
In plain words: The profits it has been saving.
Treasury stockShares of the company itself that it has bought and holds. They are subtracted from shareholders' equity and do not receive dividends.
In plain words: Its own shares kept in the drawer.
Net debtTotal financial debt minus cash and short-term investments.
In plain words: What it would really owe if it used its money to pay the debts.
Dividend per shareTotal dividends distributed divided by the number of shares.
In plain words: What you collect per year for each share.
Payout ratioDividends paid divided by net income, as a percentage.
In plain words: What part of the profit it pays out. Very close to or above 100% is hard to sustain.
ROA (return on assets)Net income divided by total assets.
In plain words: How much it earns with everything it owns.
ROIC (return on invested capital)Operating profit after taxes divided by invested capital (debt plus equity).
In plain words: How much all the money put into the business earns, whether it comes from shareholders or lenders.
LeverageUse of debt to finance assets. It is measured with ratios such as debt to equity.
In plain words: The more debt it uses, the more its profit rises when things go well and the more it suffers when they go badly.
Intangible assetsAssets with no physical form that have economic value: patents, acquired brands, software, licenses.
In plain words: Valuable things you cannot touch.
Deferred revenuePayments received in advance for goods or services the company has not yet delivered. They are recorded as a liability until the service is provided.
In plain words: Money collected before providing the service, for example an annual subscription.
LeasesLong-term rental contracts for assets. Current standards require them to be reflected as an asset and as debt.
In plain words: Long rentals that now count as debt.
Minority interestsThe part of the equity and profit of subsidiaries that belongs to shareholders other than the parent company.
In plain words: The part of the subsidiaries that is not the company's.
Other current assetsAssets that will be converted into cash in under a year and that are not cash, investments, accounts receivable or inventories: prepaid expenses, tax refunds, etc.
In plain words: A catch-all with small items that will be collected or used soon.
Right-of-use assetsValue of the right to use a leased asset (stores, offices, warehouses) for the duration of the lease. It is recorded as an asset under the current leasing standard.
In plain words: What it is worth to be able to use what it has rented for the years the contract lasts.
Other non-current assetsLong-term assets that do not fit the items above: long-term investments, deposits, other receivables.
In plain words: A catch-all with things that will last more than a year.
Short-term leasesThe part of long-term rental debts that falls due in the next twelve months.
In plain words: What it must pay this year for what it has rented.
Long-term leasesThe part of rental debts that falls due in more than a year.
In plain words: What it still has to pay, in future years, for what it has rented.
Deferred taxesTaxes the company will pay in the future because accounting and tax rules recognize certain expenses and income at different times.
In plain words: Taxes it will have to pay later, not this year.
Other current liabilitiesDebts and obligations due in under a year that are not accounts payable, financial debt, leases or deferred revenue: accrued salaries, taxes payable, provisions.
In plain words: A catch-all with small payments due this year.
Other non-current liabilitiesObligations due in more than a year that are not financial debt, leases or deferred taxes: pensions, long-term provisions.
In plain words: A catch-all with obligations due in more than a year.
Total equityTotal assets minus total liabilities. It adds up the equity of the company's shareholders and the part that belongs to minority partners of its subsidiaries.
In plain words: What is left if everything owed is paid, counting the minority partners as well.
Equity attributable to shareholdersThe part of total equity that belongs to the shareholders of the parent company, not counting minority interests.
In plain words: The part of the equity that belongs to the company's shareholders.
ImpairmentReduction of an asset's book value when it is estimated to be no longer worth what the balance sheet showed.
In plain words: When the company admits that something it had on the books is worth less.
Inventory turnoverCost of sales divided by average inventory. It indicates how many times a year the merchandise is renewed.
In plain words: If it falls, the merchandise takes longer to sell.
Days sales outstandingAverage number of days the company takes to collect from its customers.
In plain words: How many days it waits to collect what it sells.
Fiscal yearTwelve-month period that the annual accounts refer to. It does not always match the calendar year: Nike, for example, closes in May.
In plain words: The company's “year” for accounting purposes.
